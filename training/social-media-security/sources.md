# Research behind What Your Profile Gives Away

Research reviewed: 2026-09-21. The lessons, fictional packet, hints, answer keys, and timing are original proposals. No learner trial has been performed for this course.

## S1

**[Social Media Cybersecurity (tip sheet)](https://www.cisa.gov/sites/default/files/publications/Social-Media-Cybersecurity-Tip-Sheet-122019-508.pdf)**

Publisher: Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA), with the National Cyber Security Alliance

Published or updated: October 2019 (Cybersecurity Awareness Month 2019 edition; file dated December 2019)

Reviewed: 2026-09-21

**Supports:** Says there is no delete button on the internet, to set privacy settings and disable geotagging, to connect only with people you know and trust, to limit what you post (it names addresses, birthdays, and vacation plans as details criminals use to target you), and to report suspicious activity to the platform.

**Our application:** The lesson's list of what posts reveal, the trusted-connections point, and the report-to-the-platform step follow this sheet.

**Limits:** A one-page consumer awareness sheet from 2019. It does not cover workplace impersonation or firm accounts, and it cites usage statistics that are now out of date.

**Revisit when:** Recheck if CISA replaces the sheet or publishes newer social media guidance for organisations.

## S2

**[Manage your Online Presence](https://www.cisa.gov/resources-tools/training/manage-your-online-presence)**

Publisher: Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA)

Published or updated: undated

Reviewed: 2026-09-21

**Supports:** Says threat actors use publicly available information for social engineering and spear phishing; advises against posting travel details, schedules, and location, recommends private accounts and disabled location tags, says to connect only with people you know, and to check that accounts are not impersonations created to gain access to you.

**Our application:** The travel-post, welcome-post, and impersonation points in the explanation and the worked example draw on this page.

**Limits:** Written with a public-sector and personal-security audience in mind and points to a military-published guide for platform settings. It is advice, not evidence of how often these attacks succeed.

**Revisit when:** Recheck if CISA moves or rewrites the page, or if the linked platform guide changes.

## S3

**[Social media: how to use it safely](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/guidance/social-media-how-to-use-it-safely)**

Publisher: UK National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC)

Published or updated: Published 24 January 2019; reviewed 5 September 2022

Reviewed: 2026-09-21

**Supports:** Advises thinking about what followers need to know versus what is unnecessary but useful to criminals, using each platform's privacy settings, turning on two-step verification so a known password alone is not enough, spotting fake accounts (recently created, odd names, unusual activity from a known contact), checking with a friend by another method if their account looks compromised, and using platform reporting tools.

**Our application:** The account-security paragraph, the tells of a fake profile, and the verify-by-another-channel advice come from here.

**Limits:** Personal-use guidance that links out to platform settings pages which change often. It does not address firm policy or who inside an organisation should be told.

**Revisit when:** Recheck at the NCSC's next review of the page or if the platform links break.

## S4

**[How To Avoid Imposter Scams](https://consumer.ftc.gov/features/how-avoid-imposter-scams)**

Publisher: US Federal Trade Commission (FTC), Consumer Advice

Published or updated: undated

Reviewed: 2026-09-21

**Supports:** Describes scammers pretending to be businesses, government agencies, or people you know, using faked caller ID, real employee names, and urgency; advises not to click links or call numbers in unexpected messages, to verify the story by contacting the organisation through a phone number, website, or email you know is real, and to report at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.

**Our application:** The lesson's rule to verify through a channel you already have, and its treatment of urgency and secrecy as signals, follow this guidance.

**Limits:** Consumer-facing and US-centred. It focuses on money and personal information rather than on workplace documents, and its reporting route is US-specific.

**Revisit when:** Recheck if the FTC restructures its imposter-scam pages or changes the reporting route.

## S5

**[Report fake profiles](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a1338436)**

Publisher: LinkedIn Help

Published or updated: The page shows only a relative 'last updated' stamp (about nine months before review)

Reviewed: 2026-09-21

**Supports:** Gives the steps to report a profile (More, then Report or block, then choose 'This person is impersonating someone' or 'This account is not a real person'), lists warning signs such as little information, mismatched details, stock or celebrity photos, and pressure to move off the platform, and says the reported member is not told who reported them.

**Our application:** D3's platform-report step and the fake-profile tells in the explanation and model answer use this page.

**Limits:** One platform's process, and the menu labels change. It does not tell you when to also notify your employer.

**Revisit when:** Recheck when LinkedIn changes its reporting menus or the page address.
