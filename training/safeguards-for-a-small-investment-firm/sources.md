# Research behind What a Safeguards Program Actually Contains

Research reviewed: 2026-09-21. The lessons, fictional packet, hints, answer keys, and timing are original proposals. No learner trial has been performed for this course.

## S1

**[Fact Sheet: Final Rules, Enhancements to Regulation S-P](https://www.sec.gov/files/34-100155-fact-sheet.pdf)**

Publisher: US Securities and Exchange Commission

Published or updated: Undated; states the amendments were adopted 15 May 2024

Reviewed: 2026-09-21

**Supports:** States that amendments adopted 15 May 2024 apply to broker-dealers, investment companies, registered investment advisers, and transfer agents. They require written policies and procedures for an incident response program reasonably designed to detect, respond to, and recover from unauthorized access to customer information; oversight of service providers through due diligence and monitoring; notice to affected individuals as soon as practicable and no later than 30 days after becoming aware; and written records documenting compliance. Larger entities had 18 months and smaller entities 24 months from Federal Register publication to comply.

**Our application:** One example of what a safeguards program is expected to contain: the incident response, vendor oversight, notification, and recordkeeping rows of the gap list, and the reminder that the questionnaire deadline is not the only clock the packet firm faces.

**Limits:** A two-page summary, not the rule text, and it applies to SEC-registered covered institutions. An exempt reporting adviser, a state-registered adviser, or a firm outside the United States may face different obligations. Nothing in the lesson is legal advice; the firm's counsel decides which rules apply.

**Revisit when:** When the SEC amends Regulation S-P again or publishes staff guidance on it, or when the firm's registration status changes.

## S2

**[FTC Safeguards Rule: What Your Business Needs to Know](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftc-safeguards-rule-what-your-business-needs-know)**

Publisher: US Federal Trade Commission

Published or updated: December 2024 (date line on the page)

Reviewed: 2026-09-21

**Supports:** Explains the elements a covered financial institution's written information security program must include: a Qualified Individual to implement and supervise it; a written risk assessment; safeguards including access controls, a data inventory, encryption at rest and in transit, multi-factor authentication, secure disposal, change management, and activity logging; regular monitoring and testing (continuous monitoring, or annual penetration testing with vulnerability scans every six months); staff training; service provider oversight; keeping the program current; a written incident response plan; and annual reporting to the board. It adds a duty to notify the FTC within 30 days of a notification event affecting 500 or more consumers, and says firms holding information on fewer than 5,000 consumers are exempt from certain provisions.

**Our application:** The nine components in the lesson are drawn from this list, and the worked example and model answer use its point that each element needs an evidence trail, not only a policy statement.

**Limits:** Applies to financial institutions under FTC jurisdiction; the page lists investment advisers not registered with the SEC among its examples, and an SEC-registered adviser is generally under Regulation S-P instead. The nine-row frame is a teaching device drawn from this page, not a checklist any regulator has endorsed, and the page is guidance rather than the rule text.

**Revisit when:** When the FTC updates the page or amends the Safeguards Rule, or when the firm's registration changes which regulator's rule applies.

## S3

**[NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0: Small Business Quick-Start Guide (SP 1300)](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/1300/final)**

Publisher: National Institute of Standards and Technology

Published or updated: February 2024

Reviewed: 2026-09-21

**Supports:** A supplement to CSF 2.0 for small and medium businesses with modest or no cybersecurity plans, organized by the six Functions: Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, and Recover. Its actions include understanding legal, regulatory, and contractual requirements and who is responsible for the strategy (Govern); keeping an inventory of hardware, software, systems, and services (Identify); requiring multi-factor authentication on all accounts that offer it and restricting sensitive information to those who need it (Protect); monitoring logs and alerts or engaging a provider to do so (Detect); and a basic incident response plan naming a champion, who to call, and what, when, and how to report (Respond).

**Our application:** Shows that a small firm covers the nine components with people it already has; the model answer's owner and incident response rows follow its advice to name a responsible person and keep a contact list.

**Limits:** Voluntary guidance, not a regulatory requirement, and written for any small organization rather than for investment firms. It does not tell a firm which rules bind it or what a specific reviewer will accept.

**Revisit when:** When NIST revises CSF 2.0 or the quick-start guide, or when the firm grows past the point where a quick-start guide fits.
