# Research behind Verify Before You Pay

Research reviewed: 2026-09-21. The lessons, fictional packet, hints, answer keys, and timing are original proposals. No learner trial has been performed for this course.

## S1

**[Business Email Compromise: The $55 Billion Scam (Public Service Announcement I-091124-PSA)](https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2024/PSA240911)**

Publisher: FBI Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3)

Published or updated: 2024-09-11

Reviewed: 2026-09-21

**Supports:** Defines BEC as a scam targeting businesses and individuals who perform legitimate transfer-of-funds requests. Reports 305,033 incidents and $55.5 billion in exposed losses from October 2013 to December 2023 across all 50 states and 186 countries. Advises using secondary channels and/or two-factor authentication to verify requests and checking that sender addresses and URLs match. If compromised: contact your financial institution, request a recall, file a complaint at ic3.gov.

**Our application:** Lesson 1 uses the definition and scale; Lesson 2 uses the secondary-channel advice; Lesson 3 uses the immediate victim steps in the first-hour sequence.

**Limits:** Figures are complaints reported to IC3 and count exposed, not confirmed, losses, and stop at December 2023. The PSA does not prescribe cooling periods, dual approval, or callback procedure; those are this course's proposals. Reporting routes are for the United States.

**Revisit when:** When IC3 publishes a newer BEC announcement or annual report, or when the recommended victim steps change.

## S2

**[Cybersecurity for Small Business (Business Email Imposters section)](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/small-businesses/cybersecurity)**

Publisher: US Federal Trade Commission

Published or updated: 2025-09 (month only, as shown on the page when opened)

Reviewed: 2026-09-21

**Supports:** Describes a scammer setting up an email address that looks like it is from your company and sending messages from it. Recommends email authentication, prompt patching, staff training, and internal procedures that require confirmation of sensitive requests, such as calling to verify wire transfer instructions received by email. If spoofed: report to local law enforcement, the FBI's IC3, and the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov; notify customers without hyperlinks.

**Our application:** Lesson 2 uses the confirm-by-phone procedure; Lesson 3 uses the reporting routes and the instruction to alert staff and refresh training.

**Limits:** Framed mainly around your own domain being spoofed against your customers, not a vendor's compromised mailbox or a hijacked internal thread. The verification advice is brief and does not specify who calls, which number, or how long changes wait. The date was read from the page as a month only.

**Revisit when:** When the FTC revises its small business cybersecurity pages or publishes business-focused deepfake guidance.

## S3

**[Scammers use AI to enhance their family emergency schemes](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2023/03/scammers-use-ai-enhance-their-family-emergency-schemes)**

Publisher: US Federal Trade Commission, consumer alert by Alvaro Puig

Published or updated: 2023-03-20

Reviewed: 2026-09-21

**Supports:** Explains that a voice can be cloned from a short audio clip found online. Advises: do not trust the voice; call the person who supposedly contacted you and verify the story, using a number you already have; if you cannot reach them, check with someone else who knows them. Notes that scammers ask for payment by methods that are hard to reverse.

**Our application:** Lesson 2 uses the do-not-trust-the-voice rule and the callback to a known number for the D3 call; the reversibility point supports treating wires as high stakes.

**Limits:** A consumer alert about family emergency scams, not workplace payment fraud, written in 2023 before current voice and video tools. It does not address video impersonation or business approval controls.

**Revisit when:** When the FTC or another primary source publishes guidance on voice or video impersonation aimed at businesses.

## S4

**[Phishing attacks: defending your organisation](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/guidance/phishing)**

Publisher: UK National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC)

Published or updated: Published 2018-02-05, reviewed 2024-02-13

Reviewed: 2026-09-21

**Supports:** Recommends making processes more resistant to phishing by verifying all important email requests with a second type of communication (SMS, a phone call, logging into an account, or confirmation by post or in person). States that no training package can teach users to spot every phishing attempt, that blaming users for clicking does not work, and that users who fear reprisals will not report mistakes promptly, if at all.

**Our application:** Lesson 1 uses the point that spotting every attempt is unrealistic; Lesson 2 uses the second-type-of-communication rule; Lesson 3 uses the no-blame reporting position.

**Limits:** General phishing guidance for organizations, not a payment-fraud procedure; it sets no thresholds, cooling periods, or approval roles. Reporting routes it points to are for the United Kingdom.

**Revisit when:** At the NCSC's next stated review of the page, or if the NCSC publishes dedicated business email compromise guidance.

## S5

**[Phishing Guidance: Stopping the Attack Cycle at Phase One](https://www.cisa.gov/resources-tools/resources/phishing-guidance-stopping-attack-cycle-phase-one)**

Publisher: CISA, with NSA, FBI, and MS-ISAC

Published or updated: 2023-10-18 (page); document marked October 2023

Reviewed: 2026-09-21

**Supports:** Describes attackers who impersonate supervisors, trusted colleagues, or IT personnel in targeted emails. Recommends user training on social engineering and on reporting suspicious messages, and DMARC with SPF and DKIM so mail systems can check the sending server and reject spoofed domains. Includes a section for small and medium-sized organizations that prioritizes awareness training.

**Our application:** Lesson 1 uses the impersonation point and the observation that authentication checks the sending server, which is why a compromised genuine account passes; Lesson 3 gives the technology team's role in checking the mailbox.

**Limits:** Focused on credential and malware phishing rather than payment fraud; it does not name business email compromise, callbacks, dual approval, or recall steps. Technical controls in it are for the technology team, not the payment approver.

**Revisit when:** When CISA updates the guidance or publishes a companion document on payment fraud.
