# 2. Verify through a second channel

Confirm a change of destination through a route the request did not hand you, then write it down.

Suggested time: 15–20 minutes. Work on paper or in a document.

**Outcome:** Write a verification plan for one change request that names the channel, the person, and the record kept.

Read the [shared fictional packet](source-packet.md). Keep it available throughout the lesson.

A second channel is one the message did not give you: the vendor's number from the record created when the relationship began, not the number in the signature. IC3 advises secondary channels to verify requests; the NCSC asks for a second type of communication. [S1](sources.md#s1), [S4](sources.md#s4)

Two controls belong with the callback. Dual approval: a second person who did not receive the request approves any new destination. A cooling period: new bank details wait, two business days in my practice, before the first payment. The FTC calls this an internal procedure for confirming sensitive requests. [S2](sources.md#s2)

A voice that sounds like a colleague is not verification. Cloning needs a short sample, and the FTC is blunt: do not trust the voice, call the person back on a number you already have. [S3](sources.md#s3) Video is no different. Then record who you called, which number, when, and what they said.

## Worked example: Confirming D1 without asking the attacker

**Before**

Dana replies to D1: 'Thanks Ben, can you confirm the new details?' The same mailbox answers: 'Confirmed, go ahead.' Dana updates the vendor record.

**After**

Dana calls Northfield's accounts line from the 2024 vendor record and asks whether the bank has changed, without reading out the new details. She notes time, number, and answer, asks Priya to approve as second approver, and keeps NAV-2291 on the old details.

**Why:** Replying asks whoever wrote the email to confirm their own message. The callback uses a route the message never touched, and the hold means a mistake costs a late fee, not the invoice.

## Your turn

Choose D1 or D3. Write a verification plan naming the channel, the source of the contact detail, the person to reach, what you will ask, the second approver, the waiting period, and the record.

**Deliverable:** Six to ten lines that a colleague could carry out tomorrow morning without asking you anything.

- [ ] The channel and contact detail come from a record older than the request.
- [ ] The second approver did not receive the original request.
- [ ] New or changed details wait before any payment uses them.
- [ ] The record names date, time, number, person reached, and result.

## Try a changed case

Northfield's accounts line goes to voicemail. Two minutes later Ben emails offering a video call right now to confirm.

<details>
<summary>Changed-case answer</summary>

A video call arranged through the same thread is the same channel wearing a headset, and video can be imitated too. Leave a voicemail, try the switchboard number on the record, and wait. If the deadline arrives first, pay the old details.

</details>

## Check one decision

The D3 caller said not to call back and to email Marcus instead. What is the supported move?

- **A.** Release the payment; the voice was Priya's and she knew the invoice.
- **B.** Email Marcus to confirm, as she asked.
- **C.** Call Priya on her directory number anyway and hold both payments until she and a second approver confirm.

<details>
<summary>Answer and feedback</summary>

The supported choice is **C**.

**A:** A cloned voice needs a short sample, and knowing the invoice shows the caller read the same emails you did. In this packet the caller also supplied the reason not to verify.

**B:** That is the channel the request chose for you. In this packet Marcus's mailbox already carries D2, which itself needs verifying, so a reply from it settles nothing.

**C:** Supported. The number on file is a route the caller does not control, the hold costs at most a late fee, and a genuine Priya will understand. If her phone was dying, she will call back.

</details>
