# Research behind Before You Scan

Research reviewed: 2026-09-21. The lessons, fictional packet, hints, answer keys, and timing are original proposals. No learner trial has been performed for this course.

## S1

**[Scammers hide harmful links in QR codes to steal your information](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2023/12/scammers-hide-harmful-links-qr-codes-steal-your-information)**

Publisher: Federal Trade Commission, Consumer Alerts (Alvaro Puig)

Published or updated: 2023-12-06

Reviewed: 2026-09-21

**Supports:** Describes scammers covering QR codes on parking meters with their own, and sending codes by text or email with an urgent story: a package that could not be delivered, an account problem, suspicious activity. Advises checking the URL the code shows for misspellings or a switched letter, not scanning codes in unexpected messages, contacting the company through a number or website you know, keeping the phone updated, and using strong passwords and multi-factor authentication.

**Our application:** The parking-meter sticker, the urgent email, the preview check, and the 'contact them a way you already know' step in the lesson come from this alert.

**Limits:** Consumer guidance with no figures on how common QR scams are. It does not cover codes in work documents or a firm's payroll process.

**Revisit when:** Recheck when the FTC issues a newer QR code alert.

## S2

**[Cybercriminals Tampering with QR Codes to Steal Victim Funds](https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2022/PSA220118)**

Publisher: FBI Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3), Public Service Announcement I-011822-PSA

Published or updated: 2022-01-18

Reviewed: 2026-09-21

**Supports:** Warns that tampered QR codes redirect victims to malicious sites that steal login and financial information, may carry malware, and can redirect payments. Tips: check the URL after scanning, look for physical tampering such as a sticker over the original, do not download an app from a QR code, avoid paying through a site reached by code, and call the company to verify unexpected notices. Report to a local FBI field office or ic3.gov.

**Our application:** Supports the sticker check, the sign-in and payment page warning, and the 'call to verify' step. Reporting in the lesson goes to IT first, which the firm's rule (D3) requires.

**Limits:** From early 2022 and focused on payment fraud in public places. It predates the rise of QR codes in phishing emails and gives no rates.

**Revisit when:** Recheck when IC3 publishes a newer QR code announcement.

## S3

**[QR Codes - what's the real risk?](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/blog-post/qr-codes-whats-real-risk)**

Publisher: UK National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC), blog post by Dave Chismon

Published or updated: 2024-02-08

Reviewed: 2026-09-21

**Supports:** Judges QR code fraud relatively modest compared with other cyber fraud: codes in pubs and restaurants are generally safe, open areas such as stations and car parks are riskier, and the growing problem is 'quishing', codes in emails. Criminals use them because people distrust links, security tools may not scan images in emails, and personal phones lack enterprise protections. Advises using the phone's built-in scanner and caution with codes in unsolicited emails.

**Our application:** Sets the lesson's proportion (most public codes are fine), explains why codes bypass email filters and land on personal phones, and anchors the email-first worry.

**Limits:** A blog post giving the NCSC's assessment, not a measured study. UK-focused and silent on US reporting routes.

**Revisit when:** Recheck if the NCSC publishes formal QR code guidance or updates the post.
