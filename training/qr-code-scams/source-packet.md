# Three codes in one week

You work at Fennimore Grove Advisors, a small fictional firm in a shared building. Everything in this packet is fictional: the firm, the building, the people, the domains, and the notices. The exercise reference date is 22 September 2026. D1 and D2 are deliberately deceptive and should be treated as attempts to get something. D3 is the firm's guidance for the exercise.

## D1: Printed notice in the office lobby

Date: 2026-09-21 · Description of a printed notice with a QR code (fictional)

A laminated A4 notice appeared on the lobby wall beside the lift on Monday morning, next to the building's own signage and in a similar style. The heading reads: 'Parking permit renewal: action required by 26 September.' Nobody on reception was told it was coming.

The text says: 'All tenant parking permits are moving to a digital system. Scan the code below to update your permit and vehicle details. Permits not updated by the deadline will be deactivated.' Below the text is a large QR code and the line 'Managed by the building operations team.'

When you hold your phone's camera over the code without tapping, the preview banner shows permits-renewal-portal.example.net. The building manager's usual emails come from an address ending in ternbridge-house.example.com, and previous parking notices have always come by email from that address.

## D2: Email from 'payroll' with a QR code

From: Payroll Services, payroll-notices@fgadvisors-hr.example.net. To: you. Subject: 'Action needed: confirm your bank details before the September payroll run.' Received at 08:03. There is no attachment; the body holds one image, a QR code, and three short paragraphs of text.

The firm's genuine payroll messages come from finance@fennimoregrove.example.com and never ask for bank details by email; changes are made in the HR portal after signing in from a laptop. Nobody in finance has mentioned a provider migration, and the September run is not until the 30th.

## D3: Excerpt: links and codes in messages

Date: 2026-06-01 · Fictional guidance excerpt

A QR code is a link. Treat it exactly as you would treat a link in an email or text: preview where it goes before you open it, and do not open it if the destination does not match the person or organization that claims to have sent it, spelled exactly.

The firm, its payroll and HR providers, and IT will never ask you to scan a code to reset multi-factor authentication, to confirm or change bank details, or to sign in to your work account. A code that asks for any of those is something to report, not something to do.

Codes in public places are usually fine when the surface is intact, the code is printed as part of the original sign or menu, and the preview shows a domain you expect. A sticker, a notice nobody can vouch for, or an unfamiliar preview means type the address yourself instead.

Report suspicious codes and messages to it-help@fennimoregrove.example.com with a photo or a forwarded copy. Do not scan the code to check it, and do not enter anything on a page a code opened. Reporting something that turns out to be genuine is fine.
