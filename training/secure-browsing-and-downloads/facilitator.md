# Downloads, Extensions, and Pop-ups: facilitator guide

A colleague can run this course as one session of about 23–35 minutes, or as 1 short sessions. No specialist background is required to facilitate; the answer keys are in each lesson.

## Before the session

- Send or print the [fictional packet](source-packet.md) and the [worksheet](worksheet.md).
- Say plainly that everyone and everything in the packet is fictional, and that no real client, investor, employee, or credential data is used or discussed.
- Agree how answers will be shared: spoken, written, or in pairs. Reading and response alternatives are welcome.

## Ground rules

- Try the task before opening the example answer. The example is a comparison, not a mark scheme.
- Explaining a choice matters more than matching the wording.
- A first attempt with honest gaps is more useful than a polished copy of the example.

## Lesson 1: Install nothing in a hurry (8–10 minutes)

**Outcome:** A decision for each packet situation (proceed, ask first, or report) with the signal that decided it.

**Run it:** read the explanation together or ask participants to read it, walk through the worked example, then give the task. Allow most of the time for the task and the comparison.

**Discussion prompt:** Which of the three was hardest to say no to, and why?

**Changed case:** Suppose the D2 pop-up said instead 'Your computer is infected. Call this number now', with a phone number and no download. Does your decision change?

<details>
<summary>Changed-case answer</summary>

No. It is the same scam in different words: a web page cannot inspect your computer, and a real warning never asks you to call. Close the tab and report it. If you already called or let someone connect, tell the technology team now and change the passwords used on that device.

</details>

**Check:** A colleague clicked Update now in D2 and the download ran. The page now offers a free 'cleaner'. What should they do under D4? The supported choice is **B**.

<details>
<summary>Why the other options fall short</summary>

**A:** The cleaner comes from the page that showed the fake update: the second half of one scam. D4 says never run a cleaner the page offers; it hands the attacker a second install.

**C:** Nothing seeming wrong is not evidence; the installer may already have done its work. Deleting it removes the trace the technology team needs. D4 asks for a report, not a quiet cleanup.

</details>
