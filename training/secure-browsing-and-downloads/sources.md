# Research behind Downloads, Extensions, and Pop-ups

Research reviewed: 2026-09-21. The lessons, fictional packet, hints, answer keys, and timing are original proposals. No learner trial has been performed for this course.

## S1

**[Capacity Enhancement Guide: Securing Web Browsers and Defending Against Malvertising for Non-Federal Organizations](https://www.cisa.gov/sites/default/files/2023-09/Non-Fed%20-%20Guidance_for_Securing_Your_Web_Browsers%20Aug-23%20Revision.pdf)**

Publisher: Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA)

Published or updated: August 2023 revision (per the file name; the pages themselves carry no date)

Reviewed: 2026-09-21

**Supports:** Defines malvertising as malicious or hijacked adverts that spread malware, says it 'can compromise a network even if you do not click on an ad' and can force redirects, names outdated browsers and unaware users as the main openings, and recommends standardized browsers, ad blocking, protective DNS, and browser isolation. It warns that ad-blocking extensions run with high privilege and see all traffic.

**Our application:** The 'ordinary route' and 'harm without a click' points, and the approved-extension idea behind D4, follow this guide.

**Limits:** Written for IT leaders, not end users, and it does not describe lookalike domains or fake update pages specifically. The three-page guide gives no evidence on how often each route is used.

**Revisit when:** Recheck if CISA issues a newer revision or moves the file.

## S2

**[Tips to Stay Safe while Surfing the Web, Part 1: Web Browser Settings](https://www.cisa.gov/resources-tools/training/tips-stay-safe-while-surfing-web-part-1-web-browser-settings)**

Publisher: Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA)

Published or updated: undated

Reviewed: 2026-09-21

**Supports:** Says to keep the browser updated with automatic updates on and to restart it so patches apply, that pop-up notification permissions can be abused for phishing or to deliver malware, to vet browser extensions for privacy and security before installing, and not to grant site permissions such as location, camera, or microphone unless a site needs them.

**Our application:** The lesson's points on how updates really arrive, on pop-ups, and on vetting extensions before installing draw on this page.

**Limits:** A general awareness page that names one specific ad-blocking extension as a recommendation; this module does not adopt that, because extension choice is a firm decision made through the review route. No date or evidence base is given.

**Revisit when:** Recheck if CISA revises or removes the page.

## S3

**[Helping banish malicious adverts – and drive a secure advertising ecosystem](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/blog-post/helping-banish-malicious-adverts)**

Publisher: UK National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC)

Published or updated: 6 November 2024

Reviewed: 2026-09-21

**Supports:** Defines malvertising as using online advertising to deliver malicious activity, says it 'often doesn't require any user interaction', notes that ad networks let attackers target by location and device, and cites research that 0.26 percent of 1.1 trillion adverts served in 2023 were security violations, with the UK rate at 0.56 percent.

**Our application:** Supports the explanation's claim that adverts are a deliberate delivery route for criminals and that a page can do harm without a click.

**Limits:** A blog post aimed at brands and their advertising partners, not at people browsing; the statistics come from an industry study the NCSC cites rather than its own measurement.

**Revisit when:** Recheck if the NCSC publishes end-user guidance on malvertising or updates the figures.

## S4

**[How To Spot, Avoid, and Report Tech Support Scams](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/how-spot-avoid-and-report-tech-support-scams)**

Publisher: US Federal Trade Commission (FTC), Consumer Advice

Published or updated: September 2025 (month only)

Reviewed: 2026-09-21

**Supports:** Describes scams that start with fake pop-up warnings, calls, or texts claiming a virus was found, says legitimate companies do not contact you unsolicited and that real security warnings never ask you to call a phone number, and advises anyone who fell for one to change passwords, contact their bank about charges, and report at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.

**Our application:** The fake-update and 'computer is infected' points, the changed case, and the after-a-bad-install advice on passwords follow this page.

**Limits:** Consumer-facing and US-centerd; it addresses money and remote-access scams rather than workplace devices, and its reporting route is US-specific.

**Revisit when:** Recheck when the FTC updates the article or changes the reporting route.

## S5

**[Permissions requested by apps and extensions](https://support.google.com/chrome_webstore/answer/186213?hl=en)**

Publisher: Google (Chrome Web Store Help)

Published or updated: undated

Reviewed: 2026-09-21

**Supports:** Sorts extension permissions into high, medium, and low alert levels; says 'Your data on all the websites you visit' lets an extension read, request, or change data from every page; and states that a permission warning 'doesn't mean the app is dangerous, just that it can be'.

**Our application:** The lesson's reading of D3's permission line, and the point that the warning describes capability rather than intent, come from this page.

**Limits:** Chrome-specific wording; other browsers phrase the same permissions differently. It explains what a permission allows, not how to judge a particular developer.

**Revisit when:** Recheck when Google changes the permission wording or the page address.

## S6

**[Report a scam advert](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/collection/phishing-scams/report-scam-advert)**

Publisher: UK National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC)

Published or updated: Published 26 November 2021; reviewed 5 September 2022

Reviewed: 2026-09-21

**Supports:** Says criminals place adverts on websites, search engines, and social media that 'look genuine', explains that paid adverts can be reported to the Advertising Standards Authority and suspicious websites to the NCSC for takedown, and points people who lost money or were hacked to Action Fraud or Police Scotland.

**Our application:** Backs the D1 decision to report a search advert rather than only ignore it, and the idea that reporting helps the next person who searches.

**Limits:** UK reporting routes; the equivalent elsewhere differs. It does not cover how to read a domain or a download page.

**Revisit when:** Recheck at the NCSC's next review of the page or if the reporting routes change.
