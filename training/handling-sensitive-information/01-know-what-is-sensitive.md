# 1. Know what is sensitive

Sort information by who gets hurt if it leaks, not by how the file looks.

Suggested time: 15–20 minutes. Work on paper or in a document.

**Outcome:** Classify six items from the diligence folder into D3's four levels, with one sentence of reasoning each.

Read the [shared fictional packet](source-packet.md). Keep it available throughout the lesson.

I do not start with the file type or where it sits. I start with one question: who is harmed if this leaks, and how badly? The answer is a person, a company, or the firm, and it sets the level and how the item may travel.

Four levels are enough. Public can be seen by anyone. Internal is everyday working material. Confidential concerns a company or a deal and would hurt that party or us. Restricted would hurt a specific person or investor, or hand a stranger our money or systems. D3 gives the wording.

The FTC's small-business guide begins with taking stock of the personal information you hold and keeping only what you need. A diligence folder collects far more than the deal, so read it before you share it. [S1](sources.md#s1)

A spreadsheet of names looks harmless because we see names all day. The UK regulator counts disclosing personal data to the wrong person as a breach, and a list tying investors to money and contacts is personal data about each of them. Credentials let a stranger act as us. [S2](sources.md#s2)

## Worked example: The list that looked ordinary

**Before**

The LP spreadsheet can stay in the folder. Everyone on the deal knows our investors; it is only names and numbers.

**After**

Restricted. It links named investors to commitment amounts and personal email addresses; the harmed parties are those investors and our relationship with them. Under D3 it leaves the firm only with the owner's written approval, never inside a shared folder.

**Why:** The harm question points at people, not the deal; the file's plainness stops mattering.

## Try a changed case

Suppose the LP spreadsheet is edited to remove amounts and email addresses, leaving only investor names. Does its level change?

<details>
<summary>Changed-case answer</summary>

No. A list of who invested in Fund III still identifies people and organizations who expect discretion, and D3 names investor identities as restricted on their own. Removing amounts shrinks the harm, not the harmed party. It stays restricted and out of the shared folder.

## Check one decision

How should the LP spreadsheet in D2 be classified?

- **A.** Internal, because the deal team already knows the investors.
- **B.** Restricted, because it ties identifiable investors to their money and contact details.
- **C.** Confidential, but only while the amounts column is present.

<details>
<summary>Answer and feedback</summary>

The supported choice is **B**.

**A:** Familiarity inside the firm is not D3's test; the investors named are the harmed party. D2 shows the folder open to anyone with the link, so the deal team is not who can read it.

**B:** This applies D3's test: specific investors are harmed, and D3 lists investor identities, commitments, and contact data as restricted.

**C:** Names alone identify investors, which D3 places at restricted. Stripping a column does not change who is harmed or make the file safe in the open folder D2 describes.
