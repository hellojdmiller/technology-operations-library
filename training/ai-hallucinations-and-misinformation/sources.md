# Research behind When the Assistant Is Confidently Wrong

Research reviewed: 2026-09-21. The lessons, fictional packet, hints, answer keys, and timing are original proposals. No learner trial has been performed for this course.

## S1

**[Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework: Generative Artificial Intelligence Profile (NIST AI 600-1)](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.600-1.pdf)**

Publisher: National Institute of Standards and Technology

Published or updated: 2024-07 (month only)

Reviewed: 2026-09-21

**Supports:** Defines confabulation as generative AI systems generating and confidently presenting erroneous or false content, colloquially called hallucinations or fabrications; explains it as a natural result of models approximating the statistical distribution of their training data; notes that outputs may include confabulated logic or citations that purport to justify an answer, and that the risk arises when users believe false content because of the confident tone.

**Our application:** The lesson borrows the term, the explanation that fluency comes from prediction rather than lookup, and the point that a confident justification or citation can itself be fabricated.

**Limits:** A voluntary risk-management profile written for organizations deploying generative AI. It catalogues risks and suggested actions; it does not measure how often fabrication occurs or test the three checks taught here.

**Revisit when:** When NIST revises the profile or the AI RMF, or when the organization adopts a different risk framework.

## S2

**[Fabrication and errors in the bibliographic citations generated by ChatGPT](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10484980/)**

Publisher: Scientific Reports (William H. Walters and Esther Isabelle Wilder), via PubMed Central

Published or updated: 2023-09-07

Reviewed: 2026-09-21

**Supports:** Across 84 short literature reviews containing 636 citations, 55% of GPT-3.5 citations and 18% of GPT-4 citations were fabricated. Of the citations that did exist, 43% (GPT-3.5) and 24% (GPT-4) contained substantive errors such as wrong authors, titles, dates, or pagination; book chapters were especially unreliable.

**Our application:** Supports the claim that real-looking citations are often invented or wrong, and that a newer model reduces but does not remove the problem, so each citation is opened rather than trusted.

**Limits:** Two 2023 models, academic literature reviews, and bibliographic citations. It does not measure fabrication rates for current assistants, for market notes, or for statistics quoted inside a source, and it does not test the checks in this module.

**Revisit when:** When comparable studies on current models or on non-academic claims are published.

## S3

**[ChatGPT and large language models: what's the risk?](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/blog-post/chatgpt-and-large-language-models-whats-the-risk)**

Publisher: UK National Cyber Security Centre

Published or updated: 2023-03-14

Reviewed: 2026-09-21

**Supports:** States that large language models can get things wrong and hallucinate incorrect facts, producing convincing-sounding answers that may be only partly correct, especially as topics get more niche. Advises against including sensitive information in queries to public models because the provider stores and can review them.

**Our application:** Supports the point that a confident tone is not a signal of accuracy, and the closing note on keeping sensitive material out of public tools.

**Limits:** A 2023 blog post about public chat tools. It does not describe enterprise deployments with contractual data protection, and it does not measure error rates.

**Revisit when:** When the NCSC publishes updated guidance on generative AI use.
